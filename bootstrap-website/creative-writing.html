Below is my short story "Meow" which was published in Silhouette Magazine in Fall of 2024.

Thrice she meowed at me.

Recognizable and expressive, she hopped from the brick stoop to the unkempt lawn enclosed by the metal chained fence. My backpack pulled me down into a squat, its weight taking a break on my back and allowing my hand to reach out towards the unconcerned animal. She made my arm hang in anticipation as she languidly stretched under the fence through her perfectly carved out hole in the ground. How composed she was in her strut, her audacious tail lending movement to the tip and the tip only. Her catwalk could be described as nothing more than leisurely—she hadn’t bothered herself with my presence yet. When she finally took the time to greet me, I reached out and laid my hand between her shoulder blades, allowing her to move forward and arch her back into my palm. My hand against her black fur left a slight indentation and covered the reflective sun setting her coat ablaze. I noticed her collar this time and having to maneuver my hand from her chin where it was scratching, I took hold of her name tag. It read Maggie.

Twice she meowed at me.

She sat at the edge of the sidewalk, her eyes closed and her front legs representing that of columns between her thin torso. Snow had blanketed itself over her head and blotted white spots along the sides and front of her frame. There she sat, unmoving. If you looked carelessly, you’d mistake her for a trash bag—a really small trash bag. Looking beyond her at the two-story brick house, the lights swiftly shut off, drowning the landscape in a haze of gray fog. My Oregon keychain clacked against the metal zipper of my backpack as I cleared the curb of its white top-coat and carefully sat on the sidewalk next to Maggie. Eyes now opened, she looked at me. I could see her chest rising and falling at a moderate pace. Her ears flicked in rhythm with the breezy snowfall as it turned my keychain into windchimes. Then, I noticed Maggie’s tail—or, lack thereof. Instead, a puffy nub remained—half bare, half fur-covered—with the very tip of bone sticking out. I didn’t pet her this time.

Once she meowed.

It could’ve been mistaken for the wind—for the plunks of water gliding off the house’s veranda. But, I knew she’d be here, so I waited for her. The lawn was overgrown, thus it hid her body from my view until she crept under the fence, much slower than usual. Her meticulous meandering, accompanied by a soft limp, did not shake my patience because, despite my already tardy status, I waited. One, two, three. I counted three legs. Her black fur blended her body into one big void, so it wasn’t easy to tell, but Maggie’s persistent effort and failures to walk on a phantom leg confirmed my math. I waited. She only had one eye open; the other seemed sunken in behind her delicate eyelid. I waited. Her mutilated form stood in front of me, changed and yet oh so similar. Still reluctant to bat her eyes in my direction, poised in her undisputable beauty. Still eager to call at me, announce my arrival, even if it only came out in singular, gravelly vibrations. I scratched her cheek, then rubbed her ears, then caressed her back, then scooped her up into my arms and walked. Away from the gate; away from the house. Her purrs found pace with the clinks of my keychain.
